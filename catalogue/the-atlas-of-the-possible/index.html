The Atlas of the Possible

a clerk in the library of every possible image, searching for the one true picture of his own life.

Everything that could ever be drawn was already in the building, and Sereda's only task was to certify that it had been found and not made.

The distinction was the whole of his office, and like all true distinctions it was easy to state and impossible to feel at first. Nothing in the Atlas had been drawn by a hand. The Atlas held every image that could be formed on a single small grid — a panel no larger than a window's pane, ruled into a fixed and finite number of cells, each cell taking one of a fixed and finite number of greys — and because the cells were finite and the greys were finite, the arrangements, though beyond counting, were not infinite, and the building, in some manner Sereda had stopped trying to picture, contained them all. Not all that had been drawn. All that could be. The portrait of every face that had ever lived and every face that never would; the last page of every book, written and unwritten; the map of every coast, real and impossible; and between these, filling the vast and lightless majority of the drawers, the immeasurable silt of the meaningless — grey snow, static, noise, the arrangements that were arrangements and nothing more, which outnumbered the legible images as the sea outnumbers the ships upon it, by so much that the ships, statistically, did not exist at all.

A man came to the counter wanting something, and the retrievers went down into the stacks, and they brought up the drawer, and the image was in it, because the image was always in it, because everything was. And then it came to Sereda. His stamp said FOUND. He did not make images and he did not certify that they were good or true or like their subjects; he certified only that they had not been authored — that no one in the building had drawn this, that it had merely been located, lifted out of the dark where it had always been waiting, indifferent, complete. Located, not originated. He had stamped the phrase ten thousand times. It was the law of the place. In the Atlas nothing was created. There was nothing to create. It was all already there.

People came for particular things, and that was where the cruelty lived, though they rarely saw it as cruelty.

A woman came for the portrait of a child who had died before any likeness was taken. She knew the face. She carried it behind her eyes and could not get it out where another could see it, and she had heard — everyone had heard — that the Atlas held every face, and so it held his, somewhere, exactly as he had been. Sereda did not tell her the part that made it unbearable. He filled out her request, and the retrievers went down, and they came back not with one drawer but with a cart, and another cart, because the Atlas held her son's face, yes — and it held ten thousand million faces a hair's breadth from her son's, the same but for the set of the mouth, the same but for one cell of grey at the temple, a boy almost exactly her boy and not him, a boy more her boy than her memory and therefore a forgery of the truest kind, and the true one somewhere among them with no mark to single it out, no light upon it, indistinguishable from its billion near neighbours by anything the building could supply. The building could find the face. It could not tell her which face was his. That part she had to bring herself, and if she could have brought it she would not have needed the building.

She left with one. He stamped it FOUND. She wept over it at the counter and he let her, and he did not say what he knew, which was that she had not found her son; she had chosen, from an ocean of the almost-right, the one her grief consented to stop at, and the choosing had been the whole of it, and the building had only stood there holding everything, as it always did, helping no one and refusing no one and meaning nothing at all until a person came and meant something at it.

He thought about her for a long time. He thought: the building does not make meaning. The building cannot. It is the flat indifferent everything, and meaning is the thing that happens at the counter, in the choosing, in the stopping — meaning is the FOUND, which is to say the stamp, which is to say the small human act of saying this one, this is the one, I will take this and no other and call it true.

It was inevitable, given the office he held, that he would one day think of his own life.

He knew with the cold certainty of arithmetic that it was in the building. The true image of his life — call it a single panel, a single grid, the one that was somehow exactly and only his, his face or his street or the page that said what he had been — existed in the Atlas, because everything did. It had to. He could no more doubt it than doubt that the sum of two numbers waited in the world before anyone added them. Somewhere in the lightless majority of the drawers there was the picture that was the truth of Sereda, and he was, of all men, uniquely placed to go and find it.

He began at night, after the carts stopped. He did not requisition; that would have left a record, and besides there was no request to file — you could not name the thing, that was the difficulty, you could only know it when you saw it, if you could know it even then. So he went down himself, into the stacks, into the dark where the unfound settled, and he pulled drawers, and he looked. Grey snow. Grey snow. A coastline that was no coastline. Grey snow. A face that was almost a face. Grey snow for a hundred drawers, for a thousand, the static pouring past his eyes like sand through fingers, and he understood very early and refused for years to accept that he could open a drawer every second for the rest of his life and the rest of a thousand lives and not exhaust one shelf of one corridor of the building, that the true image of him was in there the way a particular grain of sand is somewhere on a particular beach on a coast that does not end — present, certain, and for all practical purposes not there at all.

And worse than the not-finding, when he made himself look at it: the forgeries. For every drawer that held the true Sereda there were a billion that held the almost-Sereda, the cruelly-wrong, the life that was his to one cell of grey and was therefore some other man's entirely, some man who had turned left where Sereda turned right and was happier, or ruined, or dead. He found a panel once that stopped his breath — a street he knew, a window he had stood at, a figure at the window that might have been him — and he held it under the lamp for an hour and could not say whether it was the truth of his life or merely the nearest of its ten thousand lies, and he had no stamp for that, no office, no law. FOUND certified only that no one had drawn it. It did not certify that it was his. Nothing could. The building held the proof and withheld the means of reading it, and this, he saw, was not a flaw in the building. This was the building telling him the truth.

He stopped going down in the dark in his nineteenth year, and he did not stop the way a man surrenders.

He stopped because he had finally understood what the woman with the dead child had understood without needing the nineteen years — that the image was never going to identify itself, that the Atlas would hold the truth of his life forever and never once say here, and that the saying-here was not the building's office but his. It had been his all along. Meaning was not in the drawers. The drawers held everything, and everything is the same as nothing; a library that contains all books contains no book; a building that holds the true picture of your life beside a billion false ones has, in the only sense that signifies, told you nothing. The meaning had never been waiting to be found. The finding was the meaning. The choosing of which of the infinite possibles to lift into the light and stamp and carry home and call your own — that small unwitnessed act at the counter, repeated, was not the path to the life. It was the life. He had spent nineteen years searching for the picture of his existence and the searching had been his existence, the nights in the stacks, the lamp, the grey snow pouring past, the one face that stopped his breath — that was Sereda, that was the true image, not waiting in a drawer but happening in the corridor, made of the looking.

So he went back to the counter. People came for their particular things, and the carts came up, and he stamped them FOUND, and he meant it now in a way he had not at the start. He was certifying, each time, the only true thing the building ever permitted: not that the image was real, not that it was theirs, only that someone had stood here and chosen it out of the everything and decided to stop. He grew old at the counter. He never found the picture of his own life, or found it a thousand times and could not tell it from its forgeries, which came to the same peace in the end; and on his last evening he went down once more, alone, and pulled a single drawer at random from the lightless majority where the unfound settled and would always settle, and he looked at what was in it — grey snow, almost certainly, the meaningless that fills the sea between the ships — and he held it under the lamp a while, and then he carried it up, and he set it on the counter, and he took his stamp, and slowly, with the care of a man who has finally learned what the word was for, he certified that it had been found.
