# Business as Usual

The tea came at eleven, as it always had, and Pelham drank it while the light over the city changed in a way no one in the office would name.

He did not know, exactly, when the changing had begun. It was the nature of the thing that it had no first day. There had been, for a season, a quality in the air that one might have called weather, and then a quality that was plainly no longer weather, and between the two there was no line a man could point to and say *here, this morning, it turned* — only the slow accumulation of mornings, each one a little further from the last ordinary one, until the office stood, as it stood now, doing its small exact work behind windows that gave onto something it had silently agreed not to look at directly. There were figures in the streets below who moved differently than figures had once moved. There were nights, now, when the city's far edge showed a colour the city had not shown before. Pelham noticed these things the way one notices a sound that has been going on too long to remark upon. He drank his tea. The forms required attention.

His work was the registry of conveyances — the recording, against the proper ledger, of the passage of small properties from one hand to another, a thing of stamps and counter-signatures and the patient reconciliation of one column against its neighbour. It had never been important and it was less important now, and he did it with the same care he had always done it, which was considerable. A conveyance came to his desk. He verified the chain of title. He stamped it, and entered it, and passed it on to Voss at the next desk, who confirmed that Pelham's entry had been correctly made, and the day proceeded down its accustomed grooves, and the tea came at eleven, and again at four, and the cart returned the completed files to the archive, where they would rest, he supposed, until there was no longer anyone to keep an archive, which he did not suppose would be very long.

---

What troubled him was not fear. He waited for fear and a kind of comfort came instead, and the comfort was the trouble.

He was, when he examined himself honestly at the eleven o'clock tea, comfortable. His days had barely changed. Outside the windows people he would never meet were losing, by every sign, everything a person could lose; and Pelham came in at the same hour, and hung his coat on the same hook, and the second hook from the left had a slight give in it that he had been meaning for years to mention and never had, and he liked that he had never had to, that the hook was still there and still gave slightly and was still his. He was warm. He was fed. The thing that was ending out there had not yet reached the registry of conveyances, or had reached it only as the changing light, the different gait of the figures below, and he found that he could sit inside his small unchanged life while the large changing thing went on beyond the glass, and that he was not suffering, and that the not-suffering, set beside what he could not help knowing was happening to others, was a more disquieting thing to carry than any terror would have been. A frightened man is at least keeping faith with the size of the event. Pelham drank his tea and was comfortable and despised, a little, the ease with which he managed it.

No one in the office spoke of any of it. This was the part that, late, he found most remarkable. There had been no meeting at which it was decided; no one had ever stood and proposed that they would carry on as before and not mention the windows; and yet every person on the floor had arrived, separately and without consultation, at the same unbroken performance, so that the agreement existed perfectly and had never once been made. They discussed the conveyances. They discussed the slowness of the cart and whether the tea had been weaker of late. When Voss's hand shook, some afternoons, holding the stamp, no one mentioned the shaking, and Voss steadied it, and the work went on, and the not-mentioning was so total, so flawlessly maintained by people who had never agreed to maintain it, that Pelham understood it for what it was: not denial, which is an effort, but something past effort, a thing the office did the way a body keeps its temperature, without willing it, because to name the cold would be to admit there was no fire, and a man cannot say that and also lift the next stamp, and the next stamp had to be lifted, so the thing went unsaid, by all of them, forever.

---

He thought once of stopping.

It was a Tuesday, or the day that had once been called Tuesday, and a conveyance lay before him recording the passage of a small house from a man to his daughter, and Pelham sat with his stamp raised and thought: there is no point in this. The house would not stand. The daughter would not — he stopped the thought, because it was the kind of thought the office did not have, but it had arrived, and he sat with the stamp in the air and felt the whole apparatus of his days reveal itself as what it plainly was, a careful pantomime conducted at the edge of an ending, a man stamping the ownership of a house onto a ledger no one would read in a city that was, by every sign past the glass, coming apart. He could put the stamp down. He could walk to the window and finally look, and say aloud the thing the floor had agreed never to say, and let the performance collapse, and that, surely, would be the honest act, the brave one, the response that kept faith with the size of what was happening.

He did not put the stamp down. He looked at the conveyance — at the man's name, and the daughter's, written by some hand in some room he would never see — and he understood that the honesty he had imagined was not honesty at all but a kind of vanity, a wish to make the ending about himself, about his own clear sight, about Pelham who had dared to look. The man who had signed the conveyance had wanted the house to pass to his daughter. He had wanted it recorded, fixed, made true in the one ledger that still recorded and fixed and made true. He had come to the registry, in the changing light, past the figures in the streets, because the registry was still open, because the stamp still fell, because somewhere a thing was still being done properly — and if Pelham put the stamp down, that man's last act of love for his daughter would rest on nothing, would not even be entered, and the daughter would not have the one small thing the world could still be made to give her, which was that it had been written down, that it had counted, that someone at a desk had taken it seriously to the end. He stamped it. He entered it. He passed it to Voss, whose hand did not shake that afternoon, and the day went down its grooves, and at four the tea came.

---

He did not arrive at peace. He arrived at something colder and steadier than peace, and he kept it the way a man keeps a coat against a wind he has stopped expecting to end.

He saw that his comfort, which had shamed him, was the very thing he had to offer. The man with the daughter, the figures in the street, Voss with his unsteady hand — they did not need Pelham to be afraid; the supply of fear was not short. What was short, what was running out faster than the light or the warmth, was the simple sight of one thing being done well and on time and without panic, the tea at eleven, the column reconciled against its neighbour, the stamp falling square on the page. His steadiness was not cowardice and it was not denial. It was the last honest thing in his keeping, the only gift left in a registry that could no longer convey anything that would last — that for as long as he sat there, a person coming to the counter would find the work being done, would find that the world had not yet entirely stopped taking them seriously, would find a man who had not looked away from his desk because looking away from his desk, in the end, was the only looking-away that he could refuse them. He owed them that. He had never been able to name to whom he owed it, only that the debt was real and that it was paid in stamps.

So he kept on. The light over the city went on changing toward whatever it was changing toward, and Pelham did not look at it directly, not from fear and not from courage but because his eyes were on the page, where they were needed; and the office maintained, by its unspoken and unbroken and never-once-proposed agreement, the performance that was no longer a performance but had become, somewhere in the long accumulation of unremarkable mornings, simply the thing they were and the thing they would be until they were not. He drank the eleven o'clock tea. He verified the chain of title. He entered the conveyance against the proper ledger, in his careful hand, so that whoever came after — if anyone came after, and he had stopped requiring that anyone come after — would find the record complete, the columns square, the work done right up to the place where the hand stopped, which is the most that has ever been asked of anyone, and was, when there was nothing else to be done, the thing Pelham did instead.
