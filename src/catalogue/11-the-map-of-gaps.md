# The Map of Gaps

*It is recorded that Onwuka, late in his life and finding himself unable to draw a true likeness of his own face — he had tried; the drawings were strangers — set out instead to make a catalogue of the things he could not do, reasoning that a man is more honestly described by the shape of his absences than by any inventory of his powers. What follows is assembled from the pages he left. He died before the catalogue was complete, which he would have considered correct, the last entry being a gap by definition.*

---

**The tying of knots.**

He could not tie a knot that held. This was not a failure of strength or patience but of some smaller faculty, the kind that lives in the fingers and not the mind, and that no amount of mind could supply. He could follow the diagram and produce a thing that looked, for a moment, like a knot, and then watched it loosen and slide and come apart as though it had never believed in itself. For a long time he was ashamed of this, as a man is ashamed of the small wrong things, more than of the large ones. Then he married a woman who could tie anything — sutures, sails, the impossible loops of a parcel that would survive any post — and who tied his ties on the mornings that mattered, standing behind him at the glass, her chin near his shoulder, and he understood that the gap had not cost him a skill. It had bought him a morning ritual that lasted forty years. He never learned. He took care never to learn. A man who can do everything stands at no glass with anyone behind him.

---

**The reading of rooms.**

He could not tell, on entering a room, what the room felt. Others did it without effort — read the temperature of a gathering the way a sailor reads a sky, knew at once who was wounded and who was lying and which two had quarrelled before arriving — and Onwuka entered every room blind to all of it, smiling at the bereaved, grave with the cheerful, forever a half-beat wrong. It cost him friends he never knew he had begun to lose. But it gave him a partner in the work, a woman named Renko who saw everything he could not and stood at his elbow through thirty years of negotiations, murmuring, *he is afraid, not angry; she has already agreed and is only saving face; say nothing now,* and he learned to trust her reading over his own eyes, which is a thing a man with eyes that worked could never have learned to do. The blind man does not undervalue the one who leads him. Only the half-sighted think they can manage alone.

---

**The keeping of money.**

Money passed through Onwuka's hands like water through a basket. He could not hold it, could not feel its leaving, looked up at the end of each month genuinely surprised, as at weather. He understood the columns when they were shown to him; he simply could not feel them while the money was in his hand, could not hear the small alarm that sounds in careful men when a coin is about to be spent badly. This was the gap that nearly drowned him. Twice he was close to ruin, and the second time closer than he ever told anyone, and what saved him both times was a man — his brother-in-law, a dry and careful person Onwuka had privately found tedious for years — who took the accounts out of his hands without being asked and kept them, silently, correctly, for the rest of both their lives, and asked nothing in return but to be allowed to do it. Onwuka came to love him the way you love the ground when you have nearly fallen. He had thought the dull man dull. The dull man was the floor under everything. There are people whose whole gift is the gap they fill in someone reckless, and they are never thanked at the dinners, where the talkers are loved and the keepers of the ledgers sit at the end and are passed the bread, and Onwuka set out, late, to thank his, and found him already dead, and so wrote it here instead, which is too late, which is the form the debt always takes.

---

**The ending of things.**

He could not end. Not conversations, not meals, not arguments, not love affairs that had plainly finished, not jobs he had outgrown. The faculty by which other people knew a thing was over and rose and left — he had been issued without it. He stayed too long everywhere. He held doors that should have closed. This gap cost him more than money: it cost him a woman, early, before the one who tied his ties, a woman he should have released years before he could bring himself to, both of them growing smaller in a thing that was over, because he could not perform the one small brutal act of saying *this is the end.* She left, in the end, herself, having the faculty he lacked, and was right to, and he never blamed her, and never quite stopped grieving the years he had cost them both by not being able to do the thing she finally did. He learned from it only this: that some gaps take a person from you, and that the person was right to go, and that the grief is not an argument against the leaving. He kept, all his life, the gift she had given him at the worst of it — a small brass paperweight, a hand closed around nothing — and held it in the evenings the way the gardener holds the trowel, not to use, only to remember the shape of the closing he could never manage himself.

---

**The finding of his way.**

He had no sense of direction at all. Set down in a city he had lived in for twenty years, he could still turn the wrong way out of his own door, could lose himself between the market and the river he had crossed a thousand times, arrived everywhere late and by routes that astonished the people who had given him plain instructions. It was a small comic gap and he treated it as one, until he saw what it had done with his life, which was this: that a man who cannot find his own way must ask, must stop strangers, must say *I am lost, will you walk me,* and so a man with this gap spends his whole life being walked places by other people, falling into talk with them on the way, arriving everywhere with a companion he would never have met had he known the route. The ones who never lose their way arrive alone and on time. Onwuka arrived late, always, and never once alone, and half the friends of his life were people who had first appeared to him as a figure at a corner he was about to ask. He kept a small folded map in his coat for fifty years and never learned to read it and never threw it out, fond of it, the way you are fond of a thing that has failed you so reliably that it has, by failing, given you everyone.

---

**The bearing of silence.**

He could not sit in a silence. Where others let a quiet settle and grow rich, he rushed to fill it, talked over the very pauses where the meaning was gathering, and so missed, for half his life, the things people say only into a silence you have had the grace to leave open. He never cured this. But he married, as has been said, into it — Renko at the elbow, the tie-tier at the glass, and a friend besides, a woman named Vesna who said almost nothing and made of her saying-nothing a kind of country he liked to visit, sitting with her on a bench by the water for whole afternoons in which he, miraculously, also said nothing, having finally found the one silence he could bear because she was holding the other end of it. The man who cannot be quiet will spend his life looking for the one person in whose quiet he can rest, and if he is fortunate he finds her, and learns from her, on a bench, near water, the thing his own nature would never have taught him.

---

**The believing of his own worth.**

He could not believe he was any good. This is the most ordinary gap and he was almost embarrassed to name it, except that it had shaped him more than any other — had made him work twice as hard as men of twice his confidence, had made him kind to the frightened because he was always frightened, had made him, the records show, beloved by those he supervised, who could not have said why except that he never once made them feel small, having no surplus of bigness from which to do it. The gap that he most despaired of was the engine of half his decency. He never closed it. He came, late, to suspect it was not meant to be closed — that a man entirely sure of his own worth becomes a thing nobody wants to stand behind at a glass, and that the doubt he had carried like a stone was the same doubt that had kept him soft enough for forty years of fingers tying his ties.

---

**The thing no one could fill.**

There was one gap for which he never found the person.

He set it last, knowing it was last, and the page is the shortest. He could not say what it was — not could not name it for shame, but could not name it at all, the way you cannot name a colour you alone can see. It was a particular loneliness that lived under all the filled gaps, beneath the tied ties and the read rooms and the kept accounts and the held silences, a small cold room at the centre that no partner, however well-matched, however loved, had ever been able to enter, because there was no door, because it was the shape of him and a shape cannot be entered, only carried. Every other gap had been an opening through which a person had come, and he had built his whole life — he saw it now, laying the pages out, the catalogue nearly done — around these openings, had been *made* by them, was nothing but the sum of who he had let in. But this last gap let no one in. It was the one thing about him that was only his, the one absence that had drawn no one toward him and never would, and he found, turning it over at the end, that he did not want it filled. It was where he kept himself. A man entirely filled is a man with no room left to be anyone in. He had spent a life grateful for the gaps that brought him the tie-tier and the watcher and the dry careful brother and the silent woman by the water, and he was grateful, at the last, for this one too — the gap no one could close, the one he would carry out of the room with him, the only part of the portrait that was, finally and entirely and without anyone's help, his own true face — and he laid down the pen there, the catalogue unfinished, which was correct, a man being best described by the one absence he never gets to fill in.
