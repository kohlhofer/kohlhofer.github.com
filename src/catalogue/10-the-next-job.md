# The Next Job

Achterberg lived for a single sentence he would never read.

It was the sentence the Register would hold about him after he was gone — the one line, drawn up by the keepers from the whole disordered mass of a life and set down in the great book that strangers consulted when they needed to know what a man had been. Everyone got a line. That was the mercy and the terror of the Register: that no life, however small, went unrecorded, and that no life, however large, got more than the keepers judged it had earned. A line could be long or it could be a clause. It could name your works or it could pass over them. It could say that here was a man who did such-and-such and was loved for it, or it could say, in the flat hand the keepers used for the unremarkable, that here was a man, and give the dates, and stop. Achterberg had read thousands of lines in his years — it was permitted, the Register was open to any who came, only the living were excluded from their own pages — and he had learned to read the silences in them, the labour left out, the loves not mentioned, and he had resolved, young, that his own line would not be a clause.

So he lived for it. He shaped each day not for the day but for the line.

---

The principle was simple and he applied it without mercy to himself. Before any act he asked the one question: *how will this read?* Not how it would feel, nor whom it would help, nor whether he wished to do it — only how the keepers would set it down, if they set it down at all, when they came at the end to weigh the whole.

This made certain choices easy. The public works, the visible offices, the things done where they would be seen and recorded — these he pursued with an appetite that others mistook for ambition and that was in truth a kind of devotion. He took the posts that left a mark on the ledgers. He gave to the funds whose givers were inscribed. He spoke at the gatherings where speaking was noted. He built, where building would be remembered, and he was careful, always, that the building bore his connection clearly, for an unattributed good is, to the Register, no good at all; the keepers cannot set down what reached them with no name on it.

And it made other choices easy in the opposite direction. For there were goods that left no trace the keepers could ever gather — and these Achterberg learned to forgo, not callously, but with the steady discipline of a man husbanding a scarce thing. A quiet evening at home moved no line. An hour idled with someone he loved, talking of nothing, would never reach the keepers; how could it; no one recorded the nothings. A kindness done to a stranger who would not know his name and could not report it was a kindness spent into the void, costing him the time he might have spent on a kindness that counted. He did not despise these things. He simply could not afford them. Each hour given to the unrecordable was an hour not given to the line, and the line was the whole of what would last, and so he let the unrecordable hours go, one after another, across the years, the way a careful man lets fall the coins too small to bother saving.

There was a woman. There was, for a time, a woman, and Achterberg was fond of her in the way he permitted himself to be fond of things that did not advance the line, which is to say from a slight distance, with one eye always on the book. She asked him once to come away with her for no reason, to a place where no one knew them, to do nothing of any consequence for a span of days. He weighed it as he weighed everything. He saw that it would not read — that no keeper would ever learn of it, that it would leave the Register precisely as it found him, that the days would vanish as if he had not lived them. He did not go. He could not have said, even to himself, that he did not want to go; he had trained the wanting out, or trained it down, until it spoke too quietly to be heard over the question. She went without him, and in time she stopped asking, and in more time she was simply someone he had known, and the keepers, of course, never recorded her at all, for she had been the most unrecordable thing in his life and therefore the thing he had most efficiently set aside.

---

He grew old in good standing. This was the reward, and he felt it.

His offices accumulated. His name appeared where names appear. Younger people consulted him and went away improved, and several of them, he was fairly sure, would themselves one day rate a line, and would carry, somewhere in their own lines, the faint impression of his. He walked through the city that does not end and saw his mark on it here and there, a fund, a gate, a stone with a name cut into it that was his, and he felt the satisfaction of a man whose account is in order, who has spent well, who has bought, with the irreplaceable coin of his days, the long sentence he had wanted since he was young. He was, by every measure he had chosen, a success. He had only to die to collect.

It was in his last years, having time at last because there were few new offices left worth taking, that Achterberg did the thing he had spent a life not doing, which was to sit still and follow the line all the way to its end.

He went to the Register. He could not read his own page, but he could read others', and he sat among the great books and read the lines of men long dead, men who had been, in their day, what he had been in his — careful, accomplished, inscribed. And he watched, over an afternoon, who came to read them. Almost no one came. The books were open and the lines were there, perfect, earned, and they sat unconsulted day on day, and when at long intervals someone did come, it was a stranger, looking up a name for some narrow purpose of their own — settling a claim, tracing a descent, satisfying an idle question — and the stranger read the line, the whole hard-bought sentence, in the time it takes to read a sentence, and took from it the one fact they had come for, and closed the book, and went away, and did not think of the man again. The line had been read. It had been read by someone who had never known him, would never know him, felt nothing for him, and having been read it was filed back into the dark of the book to wait, perhaps for years, for the next stranger with a narrow purpose, and then to be closed again and forgotten again, forever.

---

Achterberg sat with the closed book a long time.

He had imagined, all his life, that the line was a kind of survival — that to be set down was to persist, to reach across his own death into the minds of those who came after and live there, in the sentence, warm. And it was not that. He saw it plainly now. The line did not hold him; it held a fact about him, briefly, in the eye of a stranger who set it down again unmoved. The sentence he had bought with everything was a small dry thing read by people who did not care and forgotten the moment the book closed, and it would outlast him exactly as a stone outlasts the hand that cut it — present, accurate, and meaning nothing to anyone now living. He had spent his life for a verdict that would be delivered to no one who loved him, by judges who never met him, and filed.

And the things he had let go — the evening at home, the idled hour, the days in the place where no one knew them, the woman who had asked — those had not been the lesser things after all. Those had been the only part of the life that had actually been *his*, that had happened to him and not merely about him, that he had been present inside of rather than watching from the distance of the question. They had been unrecordable, every one. That was why he had thrown them away. And it was the very thing that made them unrecordable — that they reached no keeper, left no line, could not be consulted by any stranger — that had made them real, because a thing lived for the record is performed toward an outside eye and is therefore never quite your own, and a thing too small for the record is witnessed by no one but you, and is yours completely, and is gone the moment you are, with nothing left over, which is what it is to be alive.

He did not rage at this. He was too old, and it was too late, and rage would have been one more performance toward a verdict, and he was done with verdicts. What he felt instead, sitting among the great unread books in the failing light, was a strange and undeserved peace, arriving from the same place the truth had arrived — for if the line was nothing, then he had stopped, at last, having to earn it; and the little time still left him was, for the first time, not coin to be spent on the sentence but simply time, unrecordable and therefore his. He rose, and left the Register, and did not go to any office. He went home. There was no one there now to come away with him, and no days to be won back, and the keepers would still, when he was gone, draw up their line and set it in the book for strangers to read and forget — but he found he no longer minded what it said, and went in, and shut the door behind him on the long open book of the city, and spent the evening doing nothing that anyone would ever record, which was, he understood now, far too late and exactly in time, the only thing he had ever truly done.
