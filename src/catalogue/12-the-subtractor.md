# The Subtractor

Brandt had never in his life added anything to a stone, and he held that this was the only honest way to make a likeness of a man.

The others in the trade built. They took clay and pressed more clay against it, raised a brow with a coil added here, a cheek with a thumb of it there, building the face up out of nothing until it stood, and then they cast the nothing into bronze and called it the man. Brandt mistrusted all of it. A thing built up could be built wrong and then corrected, padded, patched, and the correction left no scar a layman could find, so that the finished face was a record not of a man but of the maker's hesitations, every revision still inside it, lying. Brandt worked the other way. He was given a block — marble, mostly, sometimes a close pale limestone for the poorer commissions — and he took it as a thing that already contained the face, the way every block contains every face and most of them besides, and his whole labour was to remove what was not the man until the man was left. He added nothing because nothing could be added. The figure was already there. He had only to take away the rest of the world from around it.

He was good in the way that subtraction is good, which is to say unforgiving. A man who builds may stop anywhere and resume tomorrow; the clay waits. But the stone does not give back. A cut is a cut. Brandt had learned, young, with a chisel through the lip of a magistrate's likeness he had been too eager to finish, that nothing removed can be replaced — that he could not press the chip back into the wound and have it knit, could not add the stone again, could only stand and look at the harm and begin, if the harm was great, on a new block, from the start, with the magistrate's patience thinning in the next room. So he had become slow. His slowness was famous. He took longer than any maker in the quarter and his prices reflected it and the families that wanted the dead kept true paid them, because a Brandt likeness was the man and not the maker, and that, in the end, was what they were buying.

---

The commission that undid him was a widow's, and it was for her husband, and she brought no body to work from, the husband being a year in the ground, only a small painted miniature the size of a coin and her own account of him, which she gave Brandt across three afternoons, sitting very straight, describing a face the way the grieving describe a face, which is to say not its measurements but its weather.

He had done such work before. The hardest kind. When the dead were before you, or freshly drawn, the block told you when you had arrived — the face in the stone and the face in the world agreed, and you laid the chisel down. But to carve a man no one could measure against, a man who existed now only in a coin-sized painting and a widow's certainty, was to work toward a target that moved, that lived only in the seeing, and Brandt found as he began to remove the block from around this husband that he could not say, from one day to the next, whether he had reached him.

He would cut, and step back, and the face would be there, nearly — a strong likeness, the widow said, weeping, the first time he let her see it; *that is very near him* — and Brandt heard the *near* and could not let it stand. Near was not him. Near was the world still clinging to the man in a thin coat, a hair's breadth of not-him laid over the true face, and Brandt's whole art was the removal of exactly that coat. So he cut again, a little, and the face came truer, and the widow wept harder and said yes, yes, and Brandt looked and saw, beneath the truer face, a truer one still waiting, one more skin of stone away.

---

This was the trap, and Brandt, who had spent his life avoiding the eager cut, walked into the patient one, which is worse, because it does not feel like haste.

He told himself he was being careful. Each removal was small, considered, taken only after an hour of looking; no one could call it rash. But the small removals accumulated, and the face grew finer, leaner, more exactly the husband and then more exactly than the husband, refined past the painted coin, past the widow's account, into some essence of the man that Brandt became convinced was the real one, the one the coin had been too coarse to hold and the widow too close to see. He stopped showing it to her. He could not bear her *near*; it pulled him back toward the merely accurate when he was hunting something past accuracy, the man stripped of even the accidents of his own face, the husband himself and nothing of the husband's mere appearance.

He cut, and looked, and cut. The cheeks, which had been a man's cheeks, thinned toward the cheekbone, then to the bone, the flesh of them being, Brandt saw now, not the man, only the world's padding on the man. The brow narrowed. The strong nose the widow had loved went finer, then fine, then to the blade of itself, because the breadth of it had been, he understood, an excess, a thickness of stone that was not the husband but only the place the husband had been wrapped. Each thing he took away revealed the next thing that was not yet the man, and he took that too, and the face on its plinth grew keener and stranger and more nearly nothing, and Brandt worked at it in a fever of removal, certain — wholly certain — that the truest husband was one cut further in, that he had only to reach it, that he was very close.

He was very close. He could see the place where the last of the not-man would fall away and leave the man entire, pure, the thing itself with no world on it anywhere. It was one cut off. It had been one cut off for a month.

---

He stood before it one grey morning with the chisel set against the blade of the nose, at the place where the next removal would bring the husband out at last, and he did not strike, and he could not afterward have said why, except that the stone, which all his life had told him nothing, told him something then. It told him that there was almost nothing left.

He lowered the chisel and looked, truly looked, the way he had not let himself look in weeks, and he saw what he had made. It was not the husband. It was not even a man. It was a thin keen splinter of marble, a suggestion sharpened past sense, a face refined until there was scarcely a face, every excess gone and with the excess the man, because the man had been *in* the breadth of the nose and the padding of the cheek and the ordinary thickness of a brow — the man had been in exactly the stone Brandt had spent a month removing as not-him. He had cut away the husband cut by cut, each cut reasonable, calling each thickness an untruth, and what he had reached at the end of all that removal was not the essence of the man but the absence of him, the clean perfect nothing toward which all subtraction runs if no hand stops it, because there is no bottom to taking away; you can always remove one more thing; the block goes willingly down and down to a chip, to dust, to the empty plinth, and offers no resistance anywhere, the material never once arguing for the man, because the material does not know there is a man — only the maker knows, and the maker had forgotten.

Brandt set the chisel on the bench. He did not pick it up again that day, nor for some days. He understood, standing there, the thing his whole life had nearly taught him and had withheld until now: that the difficulty had never been the removing. The removing was easy. Anyone could remove; the stone consented to any cut; a child with a hammer could reduce a master's marble to gravel in an hour and the gravel would not protest. The whole of the art, the only part that was art at all, was the stopping — the knowing, against the endless willingness of the material, the moment when the man stood revealed and one more cut would begin to unmake him. That knowing came from nowhere in the stone. It came only from the maker, from the craft in him, from the judgment that no chisel and no block could supply. He had thought his discipline was patience in the cutting. It was not. It was the far harder discipline of laying the chisel down while something still stood.

---

He could not undo what he had taken; nothing removed can be put back. The keen splinter on the plinth was past saving, and he broke it up himself rather than let it stand, and that night he was tired in a way that was almost rest.

He began the husband again, on a new block, and this time he carved toward the widow's *near* and let it be the destination rather than a station on the way to somewhere purer. When the face came up strong and a little broad in the nose, a little ordinary in the cheek, a man and not an essence, he stepped back and made himself stop, though he could feel, as he would always now feel, the further faces waiting one skin in, the endless invitation of the stone to go deeper toward the nothing it did not know was nothing. He did not accept it. He sent for the widow.

She stood a long time. Then she put her hand to the broad plain nose, the very breadth he had once cut away as untruth, and she said, *that is him,* not weeping now, only certain, and Brandt looked at the likeness she was touching and saw that it was imperfect, that the brow was a hair heavy, that a truer husband stood somewhere inside it still — and he saw, too, that the imperfect husband was present, here, on the plinth, in the room, where the perfect one could only ever have been absent; that a face, like a life, is revealed by what you strip away and ruined by stripping too far, and that the man you can put your hand on, ordinary and a little wrong and entirely there, is the only kind there is, the perfect one being merely the name the eager give to the empty plinth. He let her keep her hand on the stone. He did not reach for the chisel. He had learned the one thing the stone could never teach him, which was when to stop being taught by it, and he stood in the grey light beside the weeping widow and the imperfect, present, unfinished man, and laid the tool down, and left it down, and that was the whole of the craft, and he was at peace with it, which was more, he thought, than the perfect ever leaves anyone.
